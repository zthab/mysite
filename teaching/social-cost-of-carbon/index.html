Social planner problem: maximizing society’s welfare under externalities

Roadmap: It's easy to get lost in the next section; here I lay out what our goal is.

Ruby’s welfare tomorrow depends on everyone’s emissions, so emissions create an externality. We would like to understand how much society should be willing to pay today to avoid one additional tonne. The social planner counts the damage to every Ruby and chooses capital and mitigation to maximize total welfare. At the first-best allocation, the cost of avoiding the next tonne equals the value, in today’s consumption dollars, of the damages it prevents. That marginal damage value is the social cost of carbon.

We will do the following:

  1. Write the social welfare problem (total welfare subject to budget constraints).
  2. We make two intertemporal decisions: 1) give up consumption today to invest and get a capital return tomorrow and 2) give up consumption today to mitigate and avoid climate damages tomorrow. We will solve the social welfare problem for capital and mitigation Euler conditions, which tell us what these tradeoffs are in math.
  3. Apply a discount factor to value the benefits tomorrow in step 2 in today's dollars.
  4. Express the mitigation tradeoff per tonne: divide the current-dollar cost and benefit of a small increase in mitigation by the additional tonnes of CO₂ it avoids.

Voilà! The social cost of carbon is the benefit of avoiding one additional tonne of CO₂, expressed in today’s consumption dollars. At the first-best allocation, the planner mitigates until the cost of avoiding the next tonne equals that benefit: marginal abatement cost = SCC.

6. Papers that calculate the SCC—and papers that use it

If it's of interest, here are real papers that either calculate the social cost of carbon, or use a measure of it to characterize the costs and benefits of policies that affect carbon.

1. Calculating the SCC

  1. The Macroeconomic Impact of Climate Change: Global Versus Local Temperature

    Adrien Bilal and Diego R. Känzig (2026) · The Quarterly Journal of Economics · Working paper

    Estimates the response of world output to global temperature changes, then uses those estimates in a growth model to calculate climate damages and the SCC. Shows how measuring global rather than local temperature changes affects the damage calculation.

  2. Comprehensive evidence implies a higher social cost of CO₂

    Rennert et al. (2022) · Nature

    Builds the GIVE model with updated socioeconomic projections, climate responses, damage estimates, and discounting. A direct example of assembling the ingredients behind an SCC estimate.

  3. Country-level social cost of carbon

    Ricke, Drouet, Caldeira, and Tavoni (2018) · Nature Climate Change

    Estimates the country contributions to global marginal climate damages. Connects to our country table by showing how unevenly the damages from one tonne are distributed.

  4. Policies, projections, and the social cost of carbon: Results from the DICE-2023 model

    Barrage and Nordhaus (2024) · PNAS · Open PDF

    Calculates the SCC and optimal climate policies in a model linking capital, output, emissions, climate, and damages. Extends the logic of Ruby’s two-period planner problem to a longer horizon.

  5. Optimal Taxes on Fossil Fuel in General Equilibrium

    Golosov, Hassler, Krusell, and Tsyvinski (2014) · Econometrica · Working paper

    Derives an analytical formula for marginal carbon damage and the optimal tax under the model’s assumptions. Shows explicitly how discounting and the persistence of atmospheric carbon enter the calculation.

2. Using the SCC as an externality

  1. When Do “Nudges” Increase Welfare?

    Hunt Allcott, Daniel Cohen, William Morrison, and Dmitry Taubinsky (2025) · American Economic Review · Working paper

    Uses the SCC to value the climate externality from additional gasoline use in its fuel-economy-label experiment. Then evaluates how nudges affect welfare when consumer mistakes, taxes, and market prices also matter.

  2. A Welfare Analysis of Policies Impacting Climate Change

    Hahn, Hendren, Metcalfe, and Sprung-Keyser (2026) · American Economic Review · Working paper

  3. An Economic View of Corporate Social Impact

    Allcott, Montanari, Ozaltun, and Tan (2023, revised 2025) · NBER Working Paper 31803

    What would happen to welfare if a firm disappeared? A structural model counts consumers, workers, and substitution to other firms, and uses the SCC to value carbon emissions. A useful example of why a firm’s own emissions do not tell us its full social impact.

  4. Economic Implications of the Climate Provisions of the Inflation Reduction Act

    Bistline, Mehrotra, and Wolfram (2023) · Brookings Papers on Economic Activity

    Models how clean-energy incentives change investment and emissions, then compares the costs of reducing emissions with the SCC. Connects the per-tonne benefit derived here to the evaluation of a large subsidy package.
